The Nutrient Layer

GLP-1 Medications, B12, and Nutrient Gaps

Energy intake can fall by up to 40% on these medications. Nutrients do not get an exemption from that, and the foggy end of the symptom list is where shortfalls tend to show.

Prescribing information and studies cited at the end Published October 2026 Reading time: 6 min
Short version

What the label says

The mechanism is straightforward and the evidence is still catching up. A 2025 review found that clinical use of GLP-1 receptor agonists reduces energy intake by up to 40%, and flagged that this raises concern about adequate dietary intake and an increased risk of micronutrient deficiencies.2 The same review explicitly calls for more research, so this is a well-reasoned concern rather than a documented epidemic. B12 matters here because low B12 is an established cause of fatigue and cognitive symptoms, which makes it worth testing rather than guessing.

The arithmetic nobody mentions at the start

Weight loss is the goal, so a big drop in intake looks like success. Nutrients come along for the ride either way.

A 2025 review searching six databases to June 2025 reported that clinical use of GLP-1 receptor agonists reduces energy intake by up to 40%, producing weight loss well beyond what lifestyle changes typically achieve. The authors note that this significant reduction in food consumption raises concerns about adequate dietary intake, with an increased risk of micronutrient deficiencies.2

That is a mechanistic concern stated by researchers, and it is worth reading precisely. The review's purpose was to summarise current evidence and highlight the need for further research.2 Nobody has shown that most people on these medications become deficient. What they have shown is that intake falls far enough to make it a sensible thing to watch.

Why B12 is the one people ask about

B12 earns its reputation because the symptoms of being short of it overlap almost perfectly with how people describe life on a GLP-1: tiredness, weakness, and problems with memory and concentration. If you are foggy and eating much less than before, it is a reasonable thing to ask about.

Two cautions keep this honest. First, the strong, well-established link between a diabetes medication and B12 is with metformin, not with GLP-1 medications, and many people take both, which muddies attribution. Second, B12 is stored in the liver and shortfalls develop slowly, so a few low-appetite weeks is a different proposition from a year of greatly reduced intake. A blood test settles it, and guesswork does not.

The gaps that show up sooner

In practice, the shortfalls that make people foggy first are usually blunter than a specific vitamin:

  • Simply not enough food. The commonest driver by far, and the one covered in GLP-1 tired all the time.
  • Protein. Usually the hardest thing to eat when appetite is suppressed, and the first thing dropped.
  • Fluid. Much of it arrives with meals you are no longer having. See GLP-1 dehydration.
  • Iron. Worth considering if you were already borderline, particularly with heavy periods, since stores fall before a blood count changes.
  • Variety. When you can only manage a little, people repeat the same few tolerable foods, which narrows intake further.

What to do about it

  • Make the little you eat count. Protein and nutrient-dense foods first, filler last. This is the single highest-return change.
  • Eat on a schedule. Hunger is suppressed, so it cannot be your prompt.
  • Ask for testing rather than self-supplementing. B12, iron studies, and whatever else fits your history are a clinical decision, and a result is more useful than a guess.
  • Mention everything you take. Including metformin and any existing supplements, since they change the interpretation.
  • Ask about dietitian input. Reduced intake over months is exactly the situation dietitians are useful for.
  • Do not stop or change your medication to fix this. That is a prescriber decision.3

Where a non-stimulant option fits

A supplement cannot substitute for food, and it will not correct a nutrient gap it does not contain. For the separate question of focus, some people look at non-stimulant options rather than more caffeine. Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus) is a culinary mushroom examined in a small double-blind pilot trial tracking cognitive performance, stress, and mood in young adults.4 That is early evidence from a small field and it was not studied alongside these medications. If a nutrient gap is your problem, testing and food are the fix.

People also ask

Do GLP-1 medications cause vitamin deficiencies?

It is a flagged concern rather than an established fact. A 2025 review found these medications can reduce energy intake by up to 40% and warned this raises the risk of micronutrient deficiencies, while explicitly calling for more research. Testing is the way to know your own position.

Can low B12 cause brain fog on a GLP-1?

Low B12 is an established cause of tiredness and problems with memory and concentration, so it is worth ruling out if you are foggy and eating much less. Note that the strong medication link for B12 is with metformin rather than GLP-1 drugs, and many people take both.

Should I take a multivitamin on a GLP-1?

Ask your clinician rather than deciding alone. A multivitamin is a reasonable thing to discuss when intake has dropped substantially, but it is not a substitute for eating enough, and some nutrients, iron in particular, should not be taken speculatively.

How soon could a deficiency develop?

It depends on the nutrient and your starting stores. B12 is stored in the liver and depletes slowly, so months of greatly reduced intake matter more than a few difficult weeks. Other shortfalls, especially protein and fluid, show up much faster.

What should I ask my doctor to test?

That depends on your history, your other medications, and how long your intake has been reduced, so bring those details rather than a fixed list. B12 and iron studies are common starting points when fatigue and fog are the complaint.
