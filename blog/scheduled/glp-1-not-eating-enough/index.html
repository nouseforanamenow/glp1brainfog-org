GLP-1, Explained

Not Eating Enough on a GLP-1: The Calorie Floor Behind the Fog

The medication's job is to reduce appetite. Nothing stops it from reducing appetite further than you need, and the first thing you notice is not hunger. It is your thinking.

Prescribing information and studies cited at the end Published October 2026 Reading time: 7 min
Short version

The short version

A GLP-1 reduces appetite and slows stomach emptying, so eating much less is the expected outcome rather than a side effect.1 The problem is that nothing in the mechanism tells you where the floor is. Sustained under-eating leaves you short on fuel and on nutrients, and that tends to show up as fatigue, irritability and poor concentration rather than as hunger.2 This is the single most common everyday explanation for fog on these medicines.

  • Low appetite makes under-eating easy and easy to miss.
  • Fog, fatigue and irritability are what a too-low intake feels like.
  • Protein and overall nutrient intake usually fall together.
  • Deliberate structure replaces the hunger cues you no longer get.

The mechanism does not include a floor

This is the part that catches people out, and it is not a failure of willpower in either direction.

These medicines work by reducing appetite and slowing how fast the stomach empties, so eating considerably less is the intended effect.1 What the mechanism does not do is stop at the amount of food you actually need. For the first time in most people's lives, the signal that normally enforces a minimum has gone quiet, and the only thing left holding the floor up is deliberate structure.

Weight management guidance assumes a reasonable intake of nutrients while energy intake comes down.3 Going well below that is not a faster route to the same place. It is a different situation, and the way it announces itself is rarely a growling stomach.

What under-eating feels like from the inside

People expect hunger. What they usually report is:

  • Fatigue that rest does not fully resolve, which is widely described in the early weeks on these medicines.2
  • Difficulty concentrating and a slow, fuzzy quality to thinking, which is what brain fog describes.4
  • Irritability and a short fuse out of proportion to the day.
  • Feeling cold, light-headed on standing, or flat during exercise.
  • Hair and skin changes over longer periods, which tend to follow sustained low intake.

Because none of those shout "eat more", they get attributed to the medication itself. The medication is involved, but through this route rather than directly.

Build the floor deliberately

With appetite cues gone, the structure has to come from a plan rather than from an appetite.

  • Eat on the clock, not on hunger. Three small anchored meals work better than waiting for a signal that is not coming.
  • Lead with protein. Protein is the nutrient people most often fall short on when volume drops, and it is also the one that holds onto muscle while weight comes down.5
  • Make the food dense, not large. When volume is limited, what matters is how much nutrition each bite carries.
  • Keep fluid separate from food. Drinking a lot right before eating fills the limited space you have.
  • Write it down for a week. Most people are genuinely surprised, in both directions, and you cannot fix a floor you cannot see.
  • Ask your prescriber for a target. Many GLP-1 programmes include dietitian support, and a number set for you beats a number from the internet.

The things that stack on top

Under-eating rarely arrives alone. Fluid intake falls with food intake, because a real share of daily fluid normally comes from meals, and mild dehydration produces difficulty concentrating, headache and tiredness in its own right.67 Sleep is often disrupted in the early weeks too, and insufficient sleep degrades attention, working memory and reaction time directly.89 Fixing the food without fixing the fluid and the sleep tends to produce a partial result and a wrong conclusion.

Where supplements fit

A supplement cannot substitute for food, and this is the clearest case of that in the whole GLP-1 conversation. If intake has been low for a while, the conversation to have is with your prescriber or a dietitian about whether anything needs checking or supplementing, not with a product page.1 For people who have the food, fluid and sleep in order and are avoiding extra stimulant, Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus) is the non-stimulant option most often raised, examined in a small pilot trial covering cognitive measures, stress and mood.10 That is early, limited evidence. Supplements are regulated differently from medicines, and anything new should be cleared with the clinician managing your prescription.11

What to raise with your prescriber

Bring it up if you cannot reach a reasonable intake, if nausea is preventing you from eating at all, if you feel faint or light-headed, if weight is coming off faster than your care plan intended, or if the fog is deepening rather than settling.1 There are adjustments available, and they are theirs to make. Do not change or stop a prescribed medication on your own.

People also ask

How do I know if I am not eating enough on a GLP-1?

You usually will not feel hungry, so look at the other signals: fatigue that rest does not resolve, trouble concentrating, irritability, feeling cold, or light-headedness on standing. Writing down what you actually ate for a week is the fastest way to see it.

Why does eating too little cause brain fog?

Because fuel and nutrients are both running short, and the way that presents is as fatigue, irritability and poor concentration rather than as hunger. Fluid intake usually falls at the same time, which adds its own fog-like symptoms.

What should I eat when I have no appetite?

Smaller, denser, protein-led meals on a schedule rather than on hunger, with fluid kept separate from food so it does not fill the limited space. Your prescriber or a dietitian is the right person to set an actual target for you.

Is it normal to lose my appetite completely?

A large drop in appetite is the expected effect of these medicines. Being unable to eat at all, persistent nausea or vomiting, or losing weight faster than your care plan intended are different, and those belong in a conversation with your prescriber.

Will a supplement fix this?

No. This is the clearest case in the whole GLP-1 conversation where the answer is food rather than a product. If intake has been low for a while, ask your prescriber or a dietitian whether anything needs checking.
