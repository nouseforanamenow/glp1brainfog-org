The Body Composition Layer

GLP-1 Muscle Loss

The internet says these drugs strip muscle. The systematic reviews are more measured than that, and the disagreement itself is worth understanding before you panic or relax.

Prescribing information and studies cited at the end Published October 2026 Reading time: 6 min
Short version

What the label says

The alarming version of this story has run ahead of the evidence. A systematic review of randomised trials that measured muscle with validated imaging found tirzepatide was associated with reductions in fat mass while lean mass was relatively preserved, with muscle composition indicators stable or improved.2 Other researchers describe weight loss on these medications as producing substantial lean mass reductions, which is why trials of resistance exercise and higher protein are now running.3 Both things are in the literature, and the honest summary is that this is unsettled rather than decided.

  • A systematic review found lean mass relatively preserved.2
  • Other groups treat lean mass loss as a real risk worth trialling against.3
  • Some lean mass loss accompanies any substantial weight loss.
  • Resistance training and adequate protein are the levers being tested.3

What the systematic review found

Start with the study design, because that is what separates a finding from a headline.

A systematic review searched four major databases to June 2025 and included only randomised clinical trials that assessed skeletal muscle or lean mass using validated imaging methods in adults on tirzepatide. Its conclusion was that treatment is associated with reductions in fat mass while lean mass is relatively preserved, and that indicators of muscle composition remained stable or showed signs of improvement.2

The reviewers were careful about what that does and does not establish. They note that further research is needed to confirm the findings and to explore their functional significance over time.2 Preserved mass on a scan is not the same thing as preserved strength in daily life, and nobody has shown the latter yet.

Why you also read the opposite

Because serious researchers genuinely disagree. A randomised trial now running in Kuwait, which will enrol 232 adults starting semaglutide or tirzepatide, states in its rationale that this level of weight loss results in dramatic reductions in lean mass, reflecting loss of muscle mass and strength that can predispose people to sarcopenia.3

Note what that is: the justification for a trial, not the trial's result. It tells you the concern is taken seriously enough to fund a study. It does not settle the question, and it would be wrong to quote it as if it did. Anyone presenting either side as established is overstating what exists.

There is also a piece of context that gets lost. Losing some lean mass alongside fat is a normal feature of substantial weight loss by any method, including dieting and surgery. The live question is whether these medications make that proportion worse, not whether any lean mass is lost at all.

What this has to do with brain fog

Indirectly, but not trivially. The behaviours that protect muscle on these medications, eating enough, getting adequate protein, and staying active, are the same behaviours that keep your head clear. The usual driver of fog here is simply not eating enough, which GLP-1 tired all the time goes into. Low intake hits energy, concentration, and muscle together, so the fix overlaps.

What is being tested, and what you can reasonably do

The LEAN-P trial is randomising participants to control, resistance exercise, protein supplementation, or both, over six months. Its resistance arm is home-based, three sessions a week, progressing from one to three sets targeting major muscle groups, and its protein arm targets 1.6 g per kg of body weight per day.3 Those numbers are what researchers consider a reasonable bet, which is useful even before the results land.

  • Prioritise protein early in the day. Appetite fades as the day goes on, so later meals are the ones that get skipped. See the first weeks.
  • Include resistance work, not just walking. The intervention being tested is resistance training specifically.3
  • Do not under-eat on purpose. Eating far below your needs on top of the medication is where both muscle and clarity suffer.
  • Ask your clinic about body composition. If this worries you, it is a reasonable thing to raise and sometimes to measure.
  • Treat supplement marketing carefully. The muscle-preservation question is being studied now, so confident product claims are ahead of the evidence.

Where a non-stimulant option fits

For the separate question of daytime focus, some people look at non-stimulant options rather than more caffeine. Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus) is a culinary mushroom examined in a small double-blind pilot trial that tracked cognitive performance, stress, and mood in young adults.4 That is early evidence from a small field, it has nothing to do with muscle mass, and it was not studied alongside these medications. Clear additions with the clinician who manages your care.

People also ask

Do GLP-1 medications cause muscle loss?

The evidence is genuinely mixed. A systematic review of randomised trials using validated imaging found fat mass fell while lean mass was relatively preserved on tirzepatide. Other researchers consider lean mass loss a serious enough risk to run trials against it, so this is not settled either way.

How much protein should I eat on a GLP-1?

There is no single established number, and it should be set with your clinician or dietitian. For reference, the LEAN-P trial is testing a target of 1.6 g per kilogram of body weight per day as its protein intervention.

Does exercise prevent muscle loss on these medications?

That is exactly what is being tested rather than something already proven. The LEAN-P trial is randomising people to home-based resistance exercise three times a week, protein supplementation, or both, over six months. Resistance training is a sensible habit regardless of how the trial reads out.

Is losing some lean mass normal when losing weight?

Yes. Some lean mass is lost alongside fat during substantial weight loss by any method. The open question is whether these medications change that proportion, not whether any lean mass is lost.

Could muscle loss be causing my brain fog?

Not directly, but the common cause behind both is often the same: not eating enough. Very low intake affects energy and concentration as well as body composition, so if you are foggy and worried about muscle, intake is the first thing to look at with your clinician.
